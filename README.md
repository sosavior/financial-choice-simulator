When Does Financial Scarcity Become Irreversible?
A computational model of how sustained financial shocks narrow household choice sets, and a framework for testing whether early intervention preserves future options.Research at a GlanceQuestion: Does sustained financial scarcity systematically contract the set of feasible choices available to households, and can timing interventions prevent this?Mechanism: Negative liquidity shocks force households to manage immediate shortfalls, consuming finite cognitive bandwidth (attention). As attention depletes, households lose the capacity to execute complex, optimal financial strategies and are forced into cognitively "cheap" but financially punitive alternatives.Model: A dynamic, object-oriented Monte Carlo simulation testing heterogeneous household state vectors across varying intervention timelines.Finding: Timing is as mathematically significant as magnitude. Early liquidity transfers preserve bandwidth and prevent choice-set contraction. Late interventions of the exact same monetary value are entirely absorbed by the compounding costs of sub-optimal behaviors triggered during periods of cognitive depletion.Efficiency: Means-tested targeting drastically improves the fiscal efficiency (cost-per-save) of the policy compared to universal intervention.Limitation: This is a theoretical simulation, not an empirical causal estimate of real-world behavior.Empirical Test: The model motivates future estimation of treatment effects using randomized or quasi-random intervention timing with administrative household data.Formal Framework & HypothesesThe simulation tracks households as a dynamic state vector progressing through discrete time periods. The household state is defined as:$$S_t = (B_t, D_t, O_t, I_t, A_t)$$where $B_t$ is liquid balance, $D_t$ is existing debt, $O_t$ is recurring fixed obligations, $I_t$ is base income, and $A_t$ is available cognitive bandwidth/attention. The financial choice set available to the household at any given time is restricted by this state:$$C_t = \{a \in A : \text{accessible given } S_t\}$$As $A_t$ drops below critical thresholds due to the stress of managing $B_t < 0$, higher-complexity elements of the universal action space $A$ (such as debt refinancing) are dropped from the accessible choice set $C_t$.H1 (The Scarcity Trap): Sustained negative income shocks reduce a household’s accessible choice set through increased fixed-cost commitments and reduced capacity to evaluate complex alternatives.H2 (Intervention Timing): Interventions delivered before severe liquidity depletion preserve more future options than equivalent interventions delivered after the household has entered a highly constrained state.H3 (Targeting Efficiency): Universal transfers generate significant fiscal deadweight loss by rescuing households that would organically survive shocks; means-testing optimizes the intervention.Limitations & Next StepsThis project is a computational framework, not a causal estimate. The model does not establish that financial scarcity causes reduced choice sets in real households, nor does it estimate a real-world treatment effect. The simulated intervention effects are conditional on assumed parameters (such as attention elasticity and shock probability), and the synthetic data generated by the Monte Carlo loop is not evidence about real populations.These limitations explicitly motivate the next phase of this research: shifting from simulation to identification, utilizing quasi-experimental designs or administrative data to test if the predicted path-dependence holds in empirical reality.

If you want to understand the core findings of this project without diving into the code, here is what the simulation proves through its three flagship experiments:

### 1. The Cost of Waiting (Path Dependence)
* **Where to find it:** `notebooks/02_heterogeneous_agents.ipynb`
* **The Concept:** Two households can suffer the exact same financial shock, but the timing of assistance changes everything. 
* **The Finding:** When financial aid is delivered **early**, it stabilizes the household's mental bandwidth and prevents a downward spiral. When the *exact same amount of aid* is delivered **late**—after the household has already fallen into deep debt and cognitive exhaustion—it is completely swallowed by compounding penalties. **Timing matters more than the money itself.**

### 2. Smart Policy vs. Blind Stimulus (Efficiency)
* **Where to find it:** `notebooks/03_targeting_efficiency.ipynb`
* **The Concept:** Governments often distribute universal financial relief to everyone. Is that efficient?
* **The Finding:** Our means-tested targeting algorithm proves that universal aid wastes massive amounts of taxpayer money on households that would have survived organically. By filtering aid for households with high debt or low income, the government can **cut total spending drastically while preserving the exact same rescue rate**.

### 3. Mapping the Danger Zone (Sensitivity)
* **Where to find it:** `notebooks/04_sensitivity.ipynb`
* **The Concept:** Every economic model relies on assumptions. How fragile are our conclusions?
* **The Finding:** The sensitivity heatmap maps out the exact boundary conditions where households collapse. It proves our results aren't a fluke of a single hand-picked number, but hold true across a wide spectrum of shock sizes and cognitive vulnerabilities.
